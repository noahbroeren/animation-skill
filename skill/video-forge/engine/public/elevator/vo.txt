In 1854, a man stood high above a crowd and ordered the only rope holding him cut. Back then, if an elevator rope snapped, the platform fell, and so did everyone on it. So nobody wanted to ride one, and most buildings stopped at five or six floors of stairs. He's finished. His name was Elisha Otis. He tipped his hat, and the helper swung the axe. The rope snapped. The platform dropped, and stopped. Just a few inches down. Wait, what? Nothing was holding it up. You see, a steel wagon spring sat on top, bent tight by the rope's pull. The moment the rope went slack, the spring snapped flat, and its ends jammed into teeth on the rails. So the rope breaking is exactly what makes it stop. Three years later, a five-floor store in New York got the first passenger elevator. And Otis? He died in 1861, decades before the first skyscraper his cut rope made possible.
