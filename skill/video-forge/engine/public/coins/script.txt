# title: Why You Can't Shave a Coin
# style: toonworld-explainer
# voice: clark-v4-tight
# topic: coin clipping in 17th-century England, machine-struck coins with marked edges (1662), the Great Recoinage and Newton at the Mint (1696)
This man shaves a sliver of silver off every coin he touches, then spends the coin *anyway*.
In sixteen-hundreds England, coins are hammered by hand. No two edges match.
A snip, a file, a little pile. By Friday he has a lump of other people's silver.
Then the baker clips, the barber clips, and soon *everybody* clips.
[place:hall] By 1695, a sack of coins weighs barely *half* of what it should.
@officer: This shilling is too small.
@clipper: It shrank in the wash.
The punishment is death. People clip anyway.
So how do you stop a whole country?
[place:alley] In 1696, a new warden arrives at the Mint. Isaac Newton.
That year, every old hammered coin in England is called in and *melted*.
[beat:pivot] You see, the replacements come from a machine the Mint has used since 1662: perfectly round, with a marked edge.
Shave one, and the edge shows the cut at once.
The first time he tries, he's *caught*.
[place:street] And that is why a quarter still has one hundred nineteen ridges, guarding silver it lost in 1965.
