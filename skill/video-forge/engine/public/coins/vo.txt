This man shaves a sliver of silver off every coin he touches, then spends the coin anyway. In sixteen-hundreds England, coins are hammered by hand. No two edges match. A snip, a file, a little pile. By Friday he has a lump of other people's silver. Then the baker clips, the barber clips, and soon everybody clips. By 1695, a sack of coins weighs barely half of what it should. This shilling is too small. It shrank in the wash. The punishment is death. People clip anyway. So how do you stop a whole country? In 1696, a new warden arrives at the Mint. Isaac Newton. That year, every old hammered coin in England is called in and melted. You see, the replacements come from a machine the Mint has used since 1662: perfectly round, with a marked edge. Shave one, and the edge shows the cut at once. The first time he tries, he's caught. And that is why a quarter still has one hundred nineteen ridges, guarding silver it lost in 1965.
